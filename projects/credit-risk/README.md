# Credit card default risk: SQL analysis + Power BI dashboard

**Business question.** A card issuer sees about 1 in 5 clients default each month. Which client segments drive those defaults, and what should the issuer change in collections and credit-limit policy?

## Data
- **Default of Credit Card Clients**, UCI Machine Learning Repository (Yeh & Lien, 2009; licence CC BY 4.0). It covers 30,000 credit card clients of a bank in Taiwan, April–September 2005, in NT$.
- For each client: credit limit, age, education, the repayment status for each of the last 6 months, monthly bill and payment amounts, and whether the client **defaulted in October 2005**.
- The original `.xls` file was converted once to `data/credit_card_default.csv`, with no other changes.

## Method
| Step | File | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Load | `sql/01_create_raw.sql` | Raw table with documented column meanings |
| Clean and feature engineering | `sql/02_clean.sql` | Decodes education codes (0/4/5/6 → "Other / unknown"). Derives: credit **utilisation** (latest bill ÷ limit), **payment ratio** (September payment ÷ August bill), **months late in the last 6**, and the maximum delay. Bands each feature, and flags an early-warning **watch list**. |
| Analysis | `sql/03_analysis.sql` | Data-quality checks, default rate by segment, a status × utilisation risk matrix, and the precision and recall of the watch list |
| Dashboard | `POWERBI.md` | Three-page Power BI report built on `powerbi/clients_clean.csv`, with DAX measures |

`run.py` uses only the Python standard library and SQLite. It builds the database, runs the SQL, checks the totals against the published dataset (30,000 clients and 6,636 defaults), and writes every result to `results/*.csv`.

```bash
python run.py
```

Sex and marital status are **not used** in any segment or rule. Credit decisions should rest on behaviour, not on protected characteristics.

## Results
**Portfolio:** 30,000 clients, 6,636 defaults (**22.1%**), NT$322m of outstanding balances held by clients who defaulted.

**1. Payment behaviour is the strongest signal.**

| Latest repayment status | Clients | Default rate |
|---|---|---|
| Paid in full / no use | 8,445 | 15.6% |
| Revolving (minimum paid) | 14,737 | 12.8% |
| 1 month late | 3,688 | 33.9% |
| 2+ months late | 3,130 | **69.6%** |

The default rate rises with every extra month of lateness over the last 6 months: **11.7%** for clients never late, 38.8% for 2 late months and **70.3%** for those late in all 6.

**2. Small limits and high utilisation add risk.**
- Credit limit under NT$50k: **36.1%** default, against 11.2% for limits of NT$500k or more.
- Utilisation of 100% or more: 30.1% default, against 18.3% below 30%.
- Clients who paid under 10% of their bill: 26.0% default, against 13.9% for those who paid in full.

**3. Age barely matters.** Default rates range only from 20.3% to 25.3% across age bands.

**4. A simple early-warning rule works.** Flag every account that is **one or more months late this month**:
- It flags **22.7%** of clients (6,818).
- Their default rate is **50.3%**, 2.3 times the portfolio rate, against 13.8% for everyone else.
- It catches **51.7%** of all next-month defaults.

## Recommendations
1. **Start collections on the watch list right away.** Contact the 22.7% of accounts that are late this month (reminders, payment plans) before they roll into a second month. That group holds about half of next month's defaults.
2. **Use the months-late count in limit reviews.** Freeze limit increases for clients with 3 or more late months in the last 6 (default rates above 50%), and reward clients who have never been late.
3. **Encourage paying in full rather than the minimum.** Offer auto-pay of the full balance and alerts at high utilisation, because clients who pay under 10% of their bill, or who are over their limit, default far more often.

## Limitations
- The data is from Taiwan in 2005, so these are patterns to test, not rules to copy.
- The status codes −2 and 0 are not documented by the source. Here they are read as "no use" and "revolving".
- The rates are descriptive, not a predictive model. The next step would be a logistic regression or scorecard validated on a hold-out sample.
